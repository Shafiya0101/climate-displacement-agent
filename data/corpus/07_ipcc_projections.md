# IPCC AR6 findings relevant to displacement
> Course demo note. Summarised from IPCC Sixth Assessment Report, Working Group II,
> "Climate Change 2022: Impacts, Adaptation and Vulnerability". Source: https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/wg2/

The IPCC's Sixth Assessment Report treats displacement and involuntary migration as an observed impact
of climate change, not only a projected one, and states with high confidence that climate and weather
extremes are increasingly driving displacement across all inhabited regions, with small island states
and low-lying coastal areas disproportionately affected.

On exposure, AR6 reports that around one billion people are projected to live in low-lying coastal
zones at risk from coastal-specific climate hazards by mid-century. Sea level rise is described as
committed over centuries to millennia regardless of near-term emissions, which means coastal
displacement pressure continues to build even under strong mitigation — mitigation changes the rate,
not the direction.

AR6 gives substantial weight to vulnerability rather than hazard alone. It estimates that roughly 3.3
to 3.6 billion people live in contexts highly vulnerable to climate change, and it is explicit that
vulnerability is driven by governance, inequality and development patterns rather than by exposure
alone. The practical implication for displacement analysis is that a hazard map is not a risk map: two
regions with identical flood exposure can produce very different displacement outcomes.

The report also introduces "soft" and "hard" adaptation limits. A soft limit is reached when options
exist but are not accessible for financial or institutional reasons; a hard limit is reached when no
adaptation option can avoid intolerable risk. Displacement is the outcome once hard limits are reached,
and AR6 notes that some small island and coastal systems are already approaching them. The report is
careful not to endorse specific global migration forecasts, assessing confidence in such aggregate
numbers as low because they depend heavily on socioeconomic assumptions.
