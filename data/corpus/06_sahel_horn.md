# Sahel and Horn of Africa: climate stress and conflict interaction
> Course demo note. Compiled from FAO/FEWS NET drought reporting, IDMC and UNHCR material.

The Sahel and the Horn of Africa are where climate displacement is least separable from conflict
displacement. The Horn experienced consecutive failed rainy seasons in the early 2020s, described in
FEWS NET and FAO reporting as the worst drought sequence in decades across Somalia, southern Ethiopia
and northern Kenya, driving large-scale movement toward urban centres and displacement sites.

The causal chain typically runs: rainfall failure destroys pastoralist herds, which removes the asset
base that made mobility a coping strategy in the first place; households move to towns or camps; and
competition over the remaining water and grazing raises the probability of local violence. The
displacement is then recorded as conflict displacement, because that is the proximate trigger, even
where the underlying driver was climatic. This attribution problem systematically undercounts climate
displacement in conflict-affected regions and is the main reason IDMC's disaster and conflict figures
should not be treated as clean categories.

Two consequences follow for analysis. First, drought displacement is slow and cumulative, so it does
not produce the dated spike that monitoring systems are built to detect — it appears instead as a
rising baseline of arrivals. Second, pastoralist mobility is normal and seasonal, so distinguishing
distress displacement from routine transhumance requires knowing the herd loss, not the movement.

Displacement in this region is also predominantly internal or intra-regional rather than intercontinental.
Movement is overwhelmingly toward regional cities and neighbouring countries, not toward Europe, which
is at odds with how the phenomenon is usually framed in destination-country policy debate.
