# Pacific atoll states: planned relocation and mobility agreements
> Course demo note. Compiled from public material on the Australia-Tuvalu Falepili Union (2023),
> Fiji's Planned Relocation Guidelines, and the Kiribati "Migration with Dignity" policy.

Low-lying atoll states — Tuvalu, Kiribati, the Marshall Islands, Tokelau — face an existential rather
than a marginal risk. Mean elevation is typically two to three metres. The binding constraint arrives
before submergence: saltwater intrusion contaminates the freshwater lens that atoll populations depend
on, and storm surge over-wash makes soil unfit for staple crops. Habitability ends well before land
disappears, which is why sea level projections understate the timeline for displacement.

The Australia-Tuvalu Falepili Union, signed in November 2023, is the first bilateral treaty to create a
mobility pathway explicitly framed around climate change. It provides for a defined annual quota of
Tuvaluan citizens — reported at up to 280 per year — to migrate to Australia with access to
services, alongside Australian commitments on adaptation assistance and a security provision. Given
Tuvalu's population of roughly 11,000, the quota is significant in proportional terms, and the treaty
is widely treated as the template that other Pacific states may seek to replicate.

Fiji has taken the internal-relocation route instead, publishing Planned Relocation Guidelines in 2018
and a Standard Operating Procedure in 2023, and has already moved several coastal villages inland. The
village of Vunidogoloa is the most documented case. The lessons drawn from these moves are consistent:
land tenure at the destination is harder to resolve than construction; relocation severs access to
fishing grounds and ancestral burial sites, which communities weigh heavily; and moves that were
community-led have held up better than moves that were state-directed.

Kiribati's "Migration with Dignity" policy takes a third approach — investing in education and skills
so that citizens migrate as qualified workers under existing labour schemes rather than as future
claimants for emergency admission. Across all three approaches, the recurring political objection is
that planned relocation can be read as conceding the loss of statehood, which weakens a state's
negotiating position in climate finance and loss-and-damage forums.
