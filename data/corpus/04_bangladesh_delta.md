# Bangladesh: delta exposure and the Dhaka corridor
> Course demo note. Compiled from IOM, IDMC and World Bank material on Bangladesh.

Bangladesh sits on the Ganges-Brahmaputra-Meghna delta, with roughly two-thirds of its territory less
than five metres above sea level. Its exposure combines four hazards that interact: tropical cyclones
in the Bay of Bengal, monsoon river flooding, riverbank erosion, and saline intrusion into coastal
groundwater and soils.

Riverbank erosion is the most underrated driver. Unlike a cyclone, it destroys land permanently and
produces displacement that is by definition non-returnable, since there is no home site to go back to.
Saline intrusion works differently again: it does not destroy land but degrades it, cutting rice yields
and drinking water availability until agricultural livelihoods stop being viable. Households
typically respond with staged movement — first sending one member to a city, then relocating fully —
which makes the displacement statistically invisible to systems that count sudden movement events.

The dominant destination corridor is coastal district to Dhaka, with Chattogram as the secondary
destination. A frequently cited estimate holds that on the order of 2,000 people arrive in Dhaka each
day from rural areas, a substantial share of them from climate-affected districts, although attributing
any individual move solely to climate is not methodologically possible. Arrivals concentrate in
informal settlements that are themselves flood-prone, producing displacement into a second exposure.

Bangladesh is also cited as an early-warning success case. Cyclone Bhola in 1970 killed on the order of
300,000 people; comparable recent cyclones have produced death tolls in the tens or low hundreds, after
decades of investment in cyclone shelters, volunteer warning networks and evacuation planning. The
displacement count went up while the death count went down, which is the clearest illustration of why
a high evacuation-driven displacement figure must not be read as a policy failure.
