# Legal status of people displaced by climate impacts
> Course demo note. Summarised from UNHCR guidance and the UN Human Rights Committee's views in
> Teitiota v New Zealand (CCPR/C/127/D/2728/2016, 2020).

There is no "climate refugee" status in international law. The 1951 Refugee Convention defines a
refugee by a well-founded fear of persecution on grounds of race, religion, nationality, political
opinion or membership of a particular social group. Environmental degradation is not among them, and
UNHCR itself avoids the term "climate refugee", preferring "persons displaced in the context of
disasters and climate change".

The consequence is a protection gap. Someone who crosses a border because their island is becoming
uninhabitable generally has no automatic legal claim to admission or non-return, whereas someone
fleeing persecution does. Most climate-related displacement is in any case internal, which places it
under national jurisdiction and the Guiding Principles on Internal Displacement — a soft-law
instrument rather than a binding treaty.

The Teitiota case (2020) is the most important development. Ioane Teitiota, a Kiribati national,
challenged his removal from New Zealand on the grounds that sea level rise made return unsafe. The UN
Human Rights Committee found that New Zealand had not violated his right to life in that specific
case, because the danger was not sufficiently imminent. But it accepted the underlying principle: that
climate impacts can, in principle, trigger non-refoulement obligations where they expose an individual
to a real risk to life or to cruel treatment. It also observed that whole-country conditions could
become incompatible with the right to life before territory is physically submerged.

Regional instruments go further than the global regime. The African Union's 2009 Kampala Convention is
the only binding regional treaty covering internal displacement, and it explicitly includes
displacement caused by natural and human-made disasters. The Nansen Initiative and its successor, the
Platform on Disaster Displacement, produced a Protection Agenda endorsed by over 100 states, but it is
non-binding and works by encouraging temporary protection and humanitarian visa practice rather than
by creating status.
